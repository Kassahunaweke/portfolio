In our village, the queue for water did not always need people.

Sometimes the jerrycans were enough.

They stood in line for us.

Over time, our village had four drilled wells.

They were hand pumps, located at different places.

When everything was working, these wells were part of the normal rhythm of daily life.

But sometimes one pump broke.

Sometimes two.

And when that happened, the remaining wells had to serve everyone.

That was when the queue became a problem.

People brought their jerrycans and placed them in line.

The containers became placeholders.

You could leave your jerrycan there and wait.

And wait.

Children played around the queue.

People talked.

Some adults sat quietly, sometimes with their heads lowered, thinking about the many other things they had to deal with.

But getting the water itself was also work.

These were hand pumps.

Someone had to operate the handle again and again.

Up.

Down.

Up.

Down.

It took physical effort.

And slowly, water would fill the container.

There was also a man responsible for operating the well.

He opened the well in the morning.

People came with their containers.

He collected a small payment for each jerrycan.

And he controlled the operation of the pump throughout the day.

But there was a limit.

Darkness.

When evening came, the well had to close.

And sometimes the queue was still long.

This was the part that stayed with me.

Imagine spending most of the day waiting.

Your jerrycan is still empty.

You look ahead.

There are still people waiting.

The sun begins to go down.

Then the well man starts preparing to close.

For some people, the day was over before their turn arrived.

They had waited.

They had spent the time.

They had carried the empty container there.

And now they had to carry it back home.

Empty.

No water.

They would have to try again the next day.

As a child, I did not think of this as a problem of groundwater management.

I didn't know anything about pumping capacity.

I didn't know how many people a well could realistically serve.

I didn't know that a groundwater source could exist underground while access to that water remained limited by infrastructure.

I only knew that sometimes you could spend the whole day waiting for water and still return home without it.

Years later, studying hydrogeology changed the way I understood that memory.

The groundwater might be there.

But access to it depends on more than its presence underground.

It depends on the well.

The pump.

The pumping capacity.

Whether the infrastructure is functioning.

How many people depend on the same source.

And how long the system can operate each day.

That childhood queue was therefore more than a line of jerrycans.

It was a visible expression of a hidden system.

A system connecting groundwater beneath the ground with people above it.

And when one part of that system failed, people felt it immediately.

Today, when I think about groundwater availability, I sometimes return to that image.

A long line of jerrycans.

Children playing beside them.

Adults waiting.

The sound of the hand pump.

And eventually, the sun going down.

Some people going home with full containers.

Others carrying theirs back empty.

I used to think we were waiting for water.

Now I realize we were also waiting for something else:

**a working pump, enough capacity, enough time, and our turn.**

And sometimes, when the pump closed before we reached it,

we were simply waiting for tomorrow.
