The river may still be there.

The water may still be flowing.

But what happens when going to the river becomes dangerous?

This is a reality faced by some communities living in areas affected by armed conflict.

I have thought about this because, when we talk about water access, we often imagine a very simple problem.

There is a community.

There is a water source.

There is a distance between them.

Measure the distance.

Build infrastructure.

Provide water.

But human life is not always that simple.

Imagine waking up knowing that your family needs water.

You know where the river is.

You know the path.

You may have walked it many times before.

You take your container.

Then you stop.

Because the question is no longer:

**“How far is the river?”**

The question becomes:

**“Is it safe to go there?”**

That changes everything.

A river can remain physically close while becoming practically inaccessible.

The path has not necessarily become longer.

The water has not necessarily disappeared.

But the risk associated with reaching it has changed.

For communities living through conflict, ordinary activities can become difficult or dangerous.

Collecting water is one of them.

And there is something important about this that is easy to miss when we look only at maps.

On a map, a river is a blue line.

A settlement is a point.

The distance between them can be measured.

But a map does not automatically tell us whether a person can safely walk that route.

It does not show fear.

It does not show uncertainty.

It does not show a parent deciding whether the need for water is worth the danger of making the journey.

This is why I think about water access differently now.

When I was younger, I thought about water mainly as something that had to be found.

Later, I learned to think about groundwater availability, wells, recharge and aquifers.

But there is another question that comes before all of those:

**Can people safely reach the water?**

This is not a hydrogeological equation.

It is a human reality.

And it reminds us that water security is not only about how much water exists.

A community can live relatively close to a river and still experience a serious water-access problem.

Sometimes the barrier is physical.

Sometimes it is infrastructure.

Sometimes it is distance.

And in places affected by conflict, sometimes it is safety.

The river does not have to disappear for water access to disappear.

Sometimes the water is still there.

**It is the journey to the water that has changed.**
