There was a time when the river was simply part of life.

I was a child, probably around ten years old, growing up in a rural community in Ethiopia. Water was not something I thought about as a scientific resource. It was simply something we needed every day.

I remember following cattle toward the river.

When we reached the water, there was an unspoken order.

The cattle would drink downstream.

People would move upstream.

We didn't need to explain why.

We simply wanted the water before it had been disturbed by the animals.

Looking back now, I find that memory fascinating.

As a child, I did not know words such as *contamination*, *water quality*, or *pathogens*. I certainly did not understand the science of river transport.

But we already had a practical understanding of something important:

**where you take water from matters.**

Then one day, something changed.

A drilling machine arrived.

I remember the excitement around it. A machine was going into the ground, and people were expecting something to come out.

Water.

Groundwater.

For a community accustomed to going to the river, this was different.

The water was now coming from somewhere we could not see.

But the story did not end there.

Something surprised me.

Some people still preferred the river water.

They said it tasted better.

That may sound strange today. Why would someone prefer river water when a well had provided another source?

But water is not experienced only through scientific measurements.

People know water through taste.

Through habit.

Through memory.

Through trust.

The river was familiar.

It had been there for generations.

The well was new.

I didn't understand any of this at the time.

For me, it was simply the river and the new well.

But many years later, I began studying geology and eventually hydrogeology.

I learned that groundwater is not simply water stored in a giant underground lake.

It moves through sediments and geological formations.

Rain infiltrates.

Water moves through pores and fractures.

Different materials control how easily water moves.

And what happens underground can influence what eventually appears at a well.

Suddenly, some of my childhood memories looked different.

The river was no longer just a river.

The well was no longer just a hole in the ground.

And the decision to drink upstream was no longer just something people happened to do.

It was a small piece of practical knowledge about water quality.

The most interesting part is that I learned this long before I knew the scientific explanation.

That is something I have carried with me into my work.

Scientific knowledge does not always begin in a laboratory.

Sometimes it begins with people paying attention to their environment.

Sometimes it begins with a child following cattle to a river.

And sometimes, years later, that child becomes a hydrogeologist and realizes:

**I had been watching groundwater stories long before I knew what groundwater was.**
